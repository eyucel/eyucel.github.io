A curve can be a better business model than a line

A straight line says each additional customer contact changes sales by the same amount. That is sometimes too simple. A polynomial model lets the relationship bend—and the residual plots help us decide whether the bend is useful.

Today’s question: Does adding a squared term remove a meaningful pattern from the residuals, or are we just making the equation more complicated?

Example: coaching meetings and weekly sales

These simulated observations represent sales representatives. Extra coaching is helpful at first, but after a busy schedule is packed with meetings, time away from selling begins to matter.

What the curved line is saying

The fitted curve rises, then levels off and turns down. The important idea is not “curves are always better.” It is that the straight-line fit consistently misses in a U-shaped pattern.

A quadratic model uses x and x². The x² term is what allows the slope to change as x changes.

sales = b₀ + b₁(meetings) + b₂(meetings²)

Only trust the shape inside the range of meetings we observed. Do not use this curve to forecast an unrealistic number of meetings.

How to read this result

1 · Diagnose first

A smooth U-shape or upside-down U in residuals versus fitted values is evidence that a straight line misses structure.

2 · Add a purposeful term

Fit x and x² together. Keep both terms even if the individual linear coefficient is not impressive by itself.

3 · Re-check, then explain

The improved residual plot matters more than a slightly larger R². Describe how the slope changes, and stay within the observed x-range.

The data are simulated for practice. “Improved” means the residual pattern is less systematic—not that the model is guaranteed to be right for every future case.
