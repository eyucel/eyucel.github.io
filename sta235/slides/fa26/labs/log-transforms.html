A log model describes percentage-style change

Some business outcomes grow by multipliers: 10% more, 1.25 times as much, doubled. When the spread grows with the outcome, modeling the log of the outcome can turn a curved, fanning relationship into a more regular straight-line pattern.

The key shift: A usual linear model adds the same number of dollars for one more unit of x. A log-outcome model multiplies the outcome by the same factor for one more unit of x.

Example: product demos and weekly revenue

Businesses with more product demonstrations tend to have higher revenue, and the dollar-to-dollar spread is larger for the higher-revenue businesses. The simulated example has positive revenue, so logging the outcome is possible.

log(revenue) = b₀ + b₁(demos)

Adding one demo changes predicted revenue by a factor of eᵇ¹. As a percentage, that is 100(eᵇ¹ − 1)%, holding the other variables in the model fixed.

For example, if b₁ = 0.12, one additional demo multiplies predicted revenue by e0.12 ≈ 1.13: about a 13% increase. It does not mean “add $0.12.”

How to use a log transform responsibly

1 · Check the scale

Logging an outcome requires positive values. A log transform is useful when the relationship and residual spread suggest proportional, not constant-dollar, variation.

2 · Say “multiply” or “percent”

Interpret a one-unit x increase with eᵇ¹ or 100(eᵇ¹−1)%. Avoid describing a log coefficient as an added number of dollars.

3 · Back-transform with care

Exponentiating a fitted log value gives a typical predicted dollar amount. The arithmetic average can be a little higher when there is remaining variation.

The data are simulated for practice. The log transform changes the response scale; it does not prove that one extra demo causes revenue to change.
